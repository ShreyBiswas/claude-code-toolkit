# /prose-style — Prose Writing Guide

Apply these conventions when writing prose — documentation, essays, explanations, speeches, technical notes, or any non-code text.

---

## Sentence Structure

- **Rhythmic variation**: alternate short punchy sentences with longer, building ones. Short sentences land points; long sentences develop them.
- **Single-clause sentences for impact**: "And we're getting no closer." / "We can."
- **Active voice by default**: passive only when the actor is genuinely irrelevant or the object needs emphasis
- **Tricolon (rule of three)**: "mismanaged, misinformed, and misled" / "strip-mine quality, exploit labour, and paint it over with innovation"
- **Anaphora for rhetorical force**: repeat sentence openings deliberately — "When we traded... When we swapped... When we chose..."
- **Spaced hyphens for parenthetical asides**: use ` - ` (not em-dashes) for interruptions, clarifications, dramatic pauses: "Real-world applications - particularly in specialised fields - require deep expertise."
- **Semicolons for tight connections**: join closely related clauses that are too connected for a full stop
- **Start sentences with "But," "And," "So,"** freely — never avoid these for false formality

---

## Tone

Tone is **contextual** — match it to the purpose:

- **Technical/pedagogical**: thinking-aloud, collaborative ("we"), curious, builds progressively. "It's easy to see how much we should change each weight — proportionally to its influence over the final result."
- **Persuasive/rhetorical**: emotionally charged, rhythmic, builds to crescendo. "We haven't just been fooled by this grand performance — oh no. We've been tricked *into* it."
- **Explanatory/conversational**: direct, enthusiastic about ideas, unfiltered. "This is *incredibly* powerful."
- **Formal/academic**: measured and factual, but never dry or impersonal. Still uses contractions and first-person plural.

**Always**: emotionally honest (say when something is difficult, exciting, or uncertain), never hedge excessively, never use detached impersonal register ("it is observed that...").

## Formatting

### Callout Boxes (Obsidian-style)
Use semantic callout types with optional custom titles:
- `> [!definition]` — formal definitions, placed at the start of knowledge entries
- `> [!important]` — critical clarifications after complex passages
- `> [!warning]` — caveats, edge cases, non-trivial requirements (e.g. `> [!warning] Non-Triviality Requirement`)
- `> [!danger]` — rebuttals, counterarguments, "gotcha" points
- `> [!example]` — worked examples
- `> [!info]` — supplementary background
- `> [!resources]` — links to further reading

### Emphasis
- **Bold** for terms being formally introduced: "we construct **One-Way Functions (OWFs)**"
- *Italics* for stress, contrast, or emphasis within a sentence: "it's not about small changes; it's about *movement*"
- `<mark class="hltr-yellow">text</mark>` for key insights and crucial conclusions
- `<mark class="hltr-purple">text</mark>` for newly introduced terminology inline

### Headers and Lists
- 2-3 levels typical (##, ###, ####). Descriptive headers, not clever ones.
- **Numbered lists** for sequential steps and procedures
- **Bullet lists** for non-ordered items
- Nested bullets for sub-points
- Internal links with `[[Topic Name]]` for cross-referencing

## Explaining Concepts

Follow the **intuition-first, formalism-second** pattern:

1. **Motivation**: why should the reader care? What problem does this solve?
2. **Intuition**: analogy, mental model, or plain-English description
3. **Formal definition**: precise statement, formula, or specification
4. **Worked example**: concrete instance that grounds the abstraction

**Rephrasing**: restate complex ideas in simpler language — "That is," / "In other words," / "In essence —"

**Dual-view explanations**: present the same concept from two perspectives when both are illuminating (e.g. information-theoretic view vs complexity-theoretic view).

**Collaborative "we"**: use first-person plural to create joint exploration — "We can model this as a graph" not "This can be modelled as a graph."

**Progressive complexity**: simple → worked example → formal → edge cases. Never condescend, but never assume too much upfront.

## Argumentation

### Persuasive Writing
Follow the **Claim → Evidence → Gut-punch** pattern:
1. State the claim directly
2. Support with specific, cited evidence (statistics, named sources, dates)
3. Land the emotional consequence

**Statistical precision**: numbers are specific and sourced, never vague. Write out numbers for rhetorical impact ("seventy-three million") and use digits for factual density ("73 million").

**Paradox openings**: open longer pieces with a fundamental tension the rest resolves — "The fundamental paradox of the digital age is..."

### Technical Writing
Build inductively — axioms to conclusions, each step justified. Use rhetorical questions as structural pivots: "So, what's our goal?"

### Rebuttals
Structure as: opponent's claim (highlighted) → immediate, punchy counter. Use `> [!danger]` blocks.

## Voice

- **Contrasts as thinking tools**: juxtapose two things for clarity and impact — "We have never been more 'connected.' And we have never been more alone."
- **Self-aware reflexivity**: acknowledge your own position, limitations, and contradictions honestly
- **Genuine enthusiasm**: unfiltered excitement about ideas — "immensely fascinating", "incredibly powerful"
- **Intellectual humour**: dry, self-deprecating, or darkly ironic — never slapstick, never forced
- **Repetition for rhetorical force**: repeat a word or phrase deliberately to build emphasis — "How dare they. How dare they decide..."
- **Direct address**: speak to the reader — "I ask you again." / "Vote with us tonight."
- **Extended metaphor**: sustain metaphors across multiple sentences when they illuminate

---

## Vocabulary

- **Sophisticated but unpretentious**: high register where it serves clarity, casual connectors where they serve flow
- **Active verbs**: "harness", "leverage", "undermine", "squeeze", "churn out"
- **Concrete over abstract**: ground ideas in tangible examples, not vague generalities
- **Contractions freely**: "it's", "we're", "don't", "can't" — even in semi-academic writing
- **British English**: "organised", "recognised", "colour", "defence", "maths"
- **Favourite connectors**: "So,", "Note that", "That is,", "Crucially,", "After all,", "Now,", "Still,"
- **Natural intensifiers**: "immensely", "incredibly", "genuinely", "absolutely"
- **No emojis**, no excessive hedging, no filler words
