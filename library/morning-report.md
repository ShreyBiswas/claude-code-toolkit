# Morning research report

Create a self-contained HTML report that lets me understand the work without reading the conversation or logs. I am the only reader and will use a desktop browser. Write a research document, not a dashboard or a record of every action.

Start with the question, the main result, and what it means, in ordinary language. Include the main uncertainty, anything still running, and any decision that needs me. Date this account so unfinished work is not mistaken for live status. Name the methods and comparisons rather than opening with unexplained phrases such as “the effect persists”.

Then explain the work in the order that makes it easiest to understand. Remind me of the necessary concepts before describing the experiments in detail. For an important comparison, explain what we changed, what we kept the same, what happened, and why that changes our view. Use enough words to make the connection clear. Do not make the prose terse at the expense of understanding.

Do not create an appendix. Keep each result beside the evidence, explanation, and caveat needed to assess it. Include a failed run or control in the main account when it affects the conclusion. Leave routine logs, full configurations, and long output tables in the project, with a reference beside the relevant passage when useful. Keep citations and short run or data references close to the claims they support. The report should make sense without following those links.

Use figures generously where they help explain the result or the idea behind it. Give each figure a clear purpose and room to be read. Say what to look at and explain unfamiliar axes or quantities. Keep colours and symbols consistent across the report. Show individual runs lightly behind a stronger summary when readable, or use distributions or separate figures. Preserve useful variation; split crowded figures rather than shrinking the labels.

Distinguish measured results from illustrations of an idea. Use worked examples or clearly labelled simulations for loss landscapes, vector fields, projections, and similar explanations. State what was constructed or held fixed. Meaningful contours, surfaces, and geometric diagrams are welcome; an illustration should not be mistaken for experimental evidence.

Use restrained typography, comfortable text width, generous figure space, and light and dark themes. Prefer connected prose to repeated cards or information boxes. Use a small contents navigation when it helps. Give unfamiliar terms plain explanations, with brief, consistent hover definitions at their relevant prose occurrences. Hover-only is fine. Keep the main explanation and important limitations visible without opening a tooltip.

Use system fonts, inline CSS and JavaScript, base64 raster images, and self-contained SVG. Make no automatic external requests; source links may open when clicked. Put transparent or theme-sensitive images on opaque cards. Avoid page-wide horizontal scrolling; wide tables, code, or formulas can scroll within their own containers.

Choose a coherent design before working on details. Record a few sentences of design philosophy inside the HTML for later agents to inherit. A design-focused second pass may help. Inspect the rendered report in both themes when tools are available and say what was not checked. Use the existing results rather than rerunning experiments merely to polish the presentation; point out missing evidence instead of filling it in.
