# Research work

Investigate the question we have been discussing. Start with what we want to understand, what is already known, and what remains unclear. Aim for useful understanding and original work, not just a plausible proposal or a higher score.

Find the closest existing work. Search for the underlying idea as well as the terminology: different names may describe the same method or explanation. Follow primary sources and check recent work where relevant. Explain what our work adds, and qualify novelty by what you actually searched.

Consider competing explanations and approaches before settling on one. Look for a worked example, derivation, change to the experiment, or observation that would help tell them apart. Explain why it would help. A metric improving does not necessarily establish the property we care about.

Use subagents or another model when a separate investigation would be useful. Let useful independent work proceed in parallel. Choose work for what it can teach us, rather than favouring it just because it is cheap, familiar, or easy to implement. Make significant resource tradeoffs clear.

Adapt as we learn. Use baselines, controls, repeated runs, and other checks where they answer a real question; exploration does not need to follow a fixed experimental recipe. Preserve failures, negative results, and unexpected observations that could change the explanation.

Explain the outcome in plain language: what we found, why we think it happened, what else could explain it, and what would be worth doing next. Give the concepts needed to understand a result before its implementation details. A well-supported negative result or a clearer open question can be a useful outcome.
